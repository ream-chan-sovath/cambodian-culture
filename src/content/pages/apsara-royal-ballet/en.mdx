The Royal Ballet of Cambodia is one of the most elegant dance forms in the world, and every part of that elegance is deliberate: a vocabulary of the hands, refined over centuries, where grace is not the point of the gesture but the proof that it was made correctly.

The dance is also called *robam preah reach troap*, the dance of the royal treasure. It is also known as *lakhon luong*, the king’s theatre, because for centuries it belonged to the palace, which kept its training and its rules intact.[^preap-name] UNESCO proclaimed it part of humanity’s intangible heritage in 2003 and added it to its Representative List in 2008.[^unesco]

On stage it tells stories through four kinds of character, each with its own colours, costume, make-up and masks: *neang* (women), *neayrong* (men), *yeak* (giants) and *sva* (monkeys). An orchestra plays, and a chorus of women sings the story.[^unesco-roles]

<Figure name="dancing-apsaras-bayon-period.jpg" alt="A sandstone relief of two women dancing with bent knees and raised arms.">
Dancing apsaras carved in sandstone, Bayon period, late 12th to early 13th century. Honolulu Museum of Art.
</Figure>

Long before anyone photographed a Cambodian dancer, Khmer scribes wrote them down. The oldest dated inscription in the Khmer language, from Angkor Borei in Takeo province, is catalogued as K. 600 and dates to 611 CE. Among the people it lists serving a temple are seven dancers and eleven singers.[^preap-611]

Two centuries later, a 9th-century inscription at Prasat Lolei, near Angkor, gives two dancers their names: Lady Lamvan and Lady Ratnadevi.[^preap-lolei] The dance had a divine patron as well. Its first great teacher, in Khmer tradition, is Shiva, called Preah Eysaur in Khmer.[^preap-shiva]

<Figure name="angkor-wat-devata.jpg" aspect="3 / 4" position="50% 20%" alt="A carved woman with a tall three-pointed headdress stands in a niche on a sandstone wall.">
One of the carved women in Angkor Wat’s outer gallery.
</Figure>

At Angkor Wat the walls hold a crowd of carved women. Kent Davis’s inventory counts 1,796 of them, without the high towers.[^davis] Visitors tend to call them all apsaras, the dancers of heaven, but Davis’s inventory uses the broader word *devata*, divine beings, and most of them stand in their niches rather than dance.

Other carvings show dancers at work. Reliefs at Ta Prohm and Preah Khan show performances.[^preap-palace-reliefs] Still others show a scene anyone who has trained in this dance would recognise: older women moving a young dancer’s arms and legs into place.[^preap-teachers]

<ChapterGate n={2} title="The court and the world" km="រាជវាំង និងពិភពលោក" image="dieulefils-palace-dancers.jpg" />

<Figure name="royal-dancers-1928.jpg" alt="An early colour photograph of costumed dancers kneeling on a patterned floor in front of a seated performer." aspect="3 / 2">
Palace dancers in an autochrome by Jules Gervais-Courtellemont, published in *National Geographic* in 1928.
</Figure>

For most of its history the dance lived inside the palace. The troupe performed for coronations, royal weddings and funerals, and on Khmer holidays.[^unesco-ceremonies] In Phnom Penh it danced in the courtyard of the Tevea Vinichhay throne hall, and King Sisowath once took his dancers to Angkor to perform for the gods at one of the temples.[^preap-palace] In the 1860s the king had the troupe perform for French guests, and a French writer published a drawing of the scene.[^preap-1860s]

<Figure name="sisowath-dancers-elysee.jpg" alt="A sepia photograph of dancers kneeling on a lawn in front of a large seated audience.">
King Sisowath’s dancers before the French president at the Élysée Palace, Paris. The photograph appeared in Xavier Paoli’s memoir *Their Majesties as I Knew Them* (1911).
</Figure>

In 1906 Sisowath travelled to France, and the dancers went with him. In July they gave a performance in Paris at the Pré Catelan theatre, and the sculptor Auguste Rodin saw them for the first time.[^rodin-paris]

Rodin followed the troupe to Marseille, where they were to sail home, and in about a week made some 150 drawings of them, later washed with watercolour.[^rodin-drawings]

<Figure name="rodin-cambodian-dancer.jpg" alt="A watercolour sketch of a dancer in a pointed crown, one knee lifted and both arms curved outward.">
One of Rodin’s watercolours of the dancers, 1906. Israel Museum, Jerusalem.
</Figure>

<PullQuote cite="Auguste Rodin, on the Cambodian dancers, 1906">
They made the antique live in me.[^rodin-quote]
</PullQuote>

A century later, in 2006, the Musée Rodin in Paris devoted an exhibition to Rodin and the Cambodian dancers.[^rodin-2006]

<Figure name="rodin-hand-of-a-dancer.jpg" alt="A pencil and wash drawing of a single hand, fingers bent far back from the palm.">
Rodin’s study of a Cambodian dancer’s hand, 1906. Pencil and wash on paper.

In Khmer, *kbach* (<Term km="ក្បាច់" roman="kbach" show="km">A pattern or style. Used for carved and painted ornament, and for the gestures of dance.</Term>) means a pattern or a style. It names the ornament carved around temple doorways, including the flame-shaped *kbach phni tes* drawn along the edges of this page, and it names the gestures of the dance.[^kbach]

The gestures work like a vocabulary. You may have heard that the basic ones follow the life of a plant. The master teacher Pech Tum Kravel set out eight of them in his 2001 book on Khmer dance: sprouting, putting out a shoot, leaf, branch, flower, budding fruit (*kdeb*), mature fruit and ripe fruit falling. The idea later entered the Ministry of Education’s primary school curriculum. The photographs below, from the Cambodian publisher Yosothor, show each one.[^mien-theory]

<KbachExplorer />

In a dance, performers usually begin with the sprouting, shoot and leaf gestures and end with fruit, flower, ripeness and falling. Other gestures, such as walking and flying, are built from these basic ones, and gestures join into sentences: to say “I”, a dancer holds a hand shaped like a young shoot at the “tip of the breath”; to say “I want a flower”, the left hand becomes the flower and the right hand points to it. The same gesture is also danced differently by a woman, a man or a giant.[^mien-sentences]

The plant reading is newer than it looks. The researcher Paul Cravath also links the gestures to plant growth, but counts only four main ones: shoot, leaf, flower and fruit.[^mien-cravath] Dancers trained by earlier palace teachers, the Yosothor writers note, had never heard the theory. They describe it as an interpretation by a newer generation of artists, and decline to say whether it is right or wrong.[^mien-caution]

<ChapterGate n={4} title="Crown, silk and thread" km="មកុដ សូត្រ និងអំបោះ" image="moni-mekhala-ream-eysaur.jpg" imagePosition="50% 25%" />

Dancers don’t simply put on their costumes. Writing about the court ballet in 1990, Eileen Blumenthal described them “sewn into sumptuous sequined velvet and brocade” and wearing high-spired crowns.[^blumenthal-sewn] Those crowns, the *mkot*, are modelled on royal coronation crowns, and there are versions for both female and male roles. Giants and monkeys dance in masks.[^blumenthal-crowns]

<ChapterGate n={5} title="Near loss and return" km="ស្ទើរបាត់បង់ និងការវិលត្រឡប់" image="sisowath-dancers-elysee.jpg" imagePosition="50% 50%" />

Princess Norodom Buppha Devi dancing in a 1965 film by the United States Information Agency.

In the 1950s and 60s the ballet’s great patron was Queen Sisowath Kossamak. Khmer sources credit her with creating the Apsara dance in 1961, after a visit to a primary school where the head teacher had cut paper crowns like those carved at Angkor Wat. Her granddaughter, Princess Norodom Buppha Devi, was the first to dance its lead role.[^kmwiki]

Buppha Devi became the troupe’s principal dancer at 15.[^buppha-early] A decade after that film, the Khmer Rouge took power.

Between 1975 and 1979, under the Khmer Rouge, the tradition almost ceased to exist.[^unesco-kr] Ten years later, in May 1989, Chheng Phon spoke at a meeting at the Ministry of Information and Culture in Phnom Penh.

<PullQuote cite="Chheng Phon, May 1989">
In the Khmer Rouge massacres some 90 percent of Khmer artists were killed.[^sam-90]
</PullQuote>

Of about 3,000 members of the Khmer Association of Artists in 1975, he said, only around a tenth remained.[^sam-3000] A report from 1979 counted 40 survivors out of 190 ballet artists.[^sam-190]

The survivors started again. After 1979 troupes re-formed and performances resumed.[^unesco-return] Phnom Penh’s School of Fine Arts reopened on 27 January 1981 and gained university status in 1988.[^sam-school]

The dance is safer than it was in 1979, but it isn’t secure. UNESCO lists the pressures it faces: too little money, too few places to perform, competition from other media, and the pull of commercial shows for tourists.[^unesco-threats]

In April 2026 Soth Somaly told the news site *Cambodianess* that the name “Royal Ballet” should be kept for performances for the King or approved by the ministry, and that dancing it on bare ground, without a proper raised stage, disrespects the tradition. The same report describes too little class time for art in public schools, and trained graduates losing work to cheaper troupes.[^cambodianess]

Preap Chanmara, writing for Yosothor, sees one thread running from the carvings to the classroom: the way dancers are trained, and the movements themselves, have probably changed little from then until now.[^preap-continuity]
